# Irrigation and Water Management

## 1. Irrigation Methods in East Africa
While the majority of Ugandan agriculture remains rain-fed, transitioning to modern, efficient irrigation is essential for climate resilience. The Ministry of Agriculture, Animal Industry and Fisheries (MAAIF) actively promotes the Micro-scale Irrigation Program to assist smallholder farmers.
*   **Drip Irrigation:** The most water-efficient method, delivering water directly to the root zone via a network of perforated tubes. It minimizes evaporation and runoff. Highly recommended for high-value crops like tomatoes, onions, and greenhouse farming.
*   **Sprinkler Irrigation:** Simulates natural rainfall. While easier to set up for larger open fields compared to drip, it is susceptible to wind drift and higher evaporative losses, and can encourage fungal diseases if plant leaves remain wet overnight.
*   **Surface / Furrow Irrigation:** The traditional method of channeling water down crop rows. It has low capital cost but high water wastage and labor requirements. It can also exacerbate soil erosion.

## 2. Automatic and Sensor-Based Irrigation
Through IoT (Internet of Things) and modern agritech, smallholder and commercial farmers are adopting smart irrigation to optimize water use.
*   **Wetting Front Detectors & Chameleon Sensors:** Introduced by the Virtual Irrigation Academy (VIA) and MAAIF, these simple sensors help farmers visualize soil moisture and prevent over-irrigation.
*   **Automated Sensor Systems:** Fully automated setups utilize microcontrollers and capacitive soil moisture sensors to trigger water pumps and solenoid valves when soil moisture drops below a crop-specific Critical Min Threshold.
*   **AgriSense Integration:** A typical automated digital farm monitors real-time telemetry (volumetric water content, temperature) and makes decentralized decisions on whether to actuate center pumps or individual zone valves. This minimizes manual labor and cuts water waste by up to 30–40%.
*   **Challenges:** Frequent power outages and limited internet connectivity. Consequently, systems are increasingly designed to act independently on the edge, powered by solar arrays and backed by local batteries.

## 3. Plant Growth Stages and Irrigation Requirements
Water demand is not static. According to FAO guidelines, crops progress through four major stages, affecting how irrigation should be scheduled:
1.  **Initial Stage:** From planting/sowing to approximately 10% ground cover. Dominated by soil evaporation. The topsoil must be kept moist to ensure uniform germination, but deep soaking is unnecessary.
2.  **Crop Development Stage:** 10% ground cover to effective full cover. Root systems expand. Irrigation should run deeper to encourage deep root growth.
3.  **Mid-Season Stage:** Effective full cover to the start of maturity. Includes flowering and fruiting/grain-filling. This is the **most critical stage for water**. Moisture stress here drastically reduces final yield (e.g., maize silking/tasseling phase, bean pod filling, tomato fruit enlargement).
4.  **Late-Season Stage:** Maturation to harvest. Irrigation is significantly reduced and eventually cut off to allow crops to dry naturally and prevent rot.

*(Sources: FAO Crop Yield Response to Water (FAO56), MAAIF Micro-scale Irrigation Program)*
